## Anime audience and appeal checklist

Approved for the next anime short on 2026-11-16. Apply this during concept selection, script revision, direction and integrated footage review. Anime fans are the intended audience; relationships are one possible subject, not a required genre. Consider action, powers, lore, character motives, comedy, emotional reinterpretations and unusual world rules.

Before presenting a concept or script, answer these five questions concretely:

1. Why would an anime fan stop for this specific idea?
2. What question gives the viewer a reason to continue, and how does the story develop it?
3. What emotion develops through the character's choices and consequences?
4. What does our visual retelling add beyond the spoken summary or familiar source material?
5. What makes the ending worth reaching, remembering or sharing?

Evaluate the complete execution across these dimensions:

- **Recognition:** preserve recognizable character, costume, object and setting cues without requiring a lore briefing.
- **Curiosity and promise:** make a specific, accurate promise through title, packaging and opening; begin fulfilling it promptly.
- **Fresh perspective:** offer a distinctive interpretation or causal explanation, even when fans already know the events.
- **Accessibility and trust:** establish the necessary rule and stakes clearly. Verify canonical claims against the relevant source; distinguish interpretation, uncertainty and original fiction. Do not invent canon.
- **Emotional progression:** choose the appropriate engine—danger, admiration, embarrassment, injustice, cleverness, affection, grief or absurdity—and develop it through actions.
- **Escalation:** each beat answers, complicates or transforms the viewer's question. Keep background only when it supports the next meaningful event.
- **Visual contribution:** use expressive physical acting, meaningful props and distinctive environments to demonstrate the story. Preserve proven systems and compare against the strengths of prior projects.
- **Attention:** coordinate subject scale, framing, depth, lighting, captions, transitions and movement around what matters now. Extra cuts or effects do not prove impact.
- **Audio:** direct narration, pauses, music and action accents as part of the emotional progression. Establish delivery and timing through actual audiovisual review, separately from measurements.
- **Payoff:** deliver the promised explanation, reversal, consequence or emotional reinterpretation; the conclusion should reward the setup.

Draft, compare with relevant actual reference sequences, and revise internally before presenting the script. After production authorization, apply the same criteria to the integrated moving footage. Keep creative and physical verdicts separate. Screenshots cannot verify performance, pacing or sound.

These are creative hypotheses and acceptance questions, not a guarantee of virality or an invented score. Public views do not establish causation; evaluate viewing choice, retention and satisfaction with actual audience evidence when available. Do not infer that a genre is superior from one breakout. Do not impose a fixed duration, speaking rate, scene count or emotional formula solely to resemble a competitor.

## Narration structure

1. **Hook:** introduce a concrete contradiction and stakes immediately. Leave a specific question unanswered; do not reveal the method before the audience has a reason to care.
2. **Setting:** establish the necessary time and place immediately after the hook, or in it. Avoid introductory context that delays the story.
3. **Escalation:** use two or three connected actions that increase commitment, risk, or consequence. Each sentence must change the situation. Show why a reasonable person might believe the claim.
4. **Clue or reversal:** introduce a concrete observation or test the audience can understand visually. Explain what makes it meaningful rather than merely labeling it suspicious.
5. **Mechanism:** reveal the withheld cause in plain language. The explanation should resolve the opening question.
6. **Payoff:** finish with a brief consequence, irony, or callback tied to the original action and characters. Stop there; do not add an unrelated fact or explain the punchline.

Keep the narration conversational, concise, factual, and easy to visualize. Use only necessary characters, names, dates, and numbers. Do not invent dialogue or force every story into a fraud plot. When revealing the method upfront serves a different story better, the later reveal must add a meaningful new consequence rather than repeat the hook.

Display years as digits in captions (for example, 1872), even when the narration transcript spells them out. Hold the numeric year through its complete spoken phrase.

Before recording or building, ask an unbriefed listener to explain what happened, why the key decision made sense, and what changed their understanding. Check that the ending answers the opening question. A numerical rating or conformity to this structure does not establish audience impact.

## Visual hook priority

The user explicitly prioritizes the first two seconds. Start with a legible action already underway, not an introduction or a passive object display. Camera movement must reveal a relationship, evidence, or consequence while preserving orientation. Use contrast, acting, depth, and sound to direct attention; do not substitute extra cuts, camera speed, bloom, or loudness for a reason to keep watching.

For each build, cross-reference relevant actual reference openings and the repo's current camera/acting guidance. Assign a reference researcher and an independent opening reviewer when delegating; give them separate responsibilities and require concrete evidence and objections. Review a moving opening preview through the complete hook sentence before polishing the rest. First assess the action without explanatory briefing, then assess the picture with narration. Reject unclear action, unreadable props or faces, an unmotivated camera move, or a premature method reveal. These checks cannot guarantee viewer retention.

## Performance, camera, and emphasis review

### What polished means for this project

The user defines polish as coordinated direction that controls attention and makes the story felt. Perspective, performance, lighting, editing, and sound must make each important action understandable, consequential, and satisfying. A clean render is a technical prerequisite; visual finish alone is not an artistic approval.

Each story needs an explicit emotional arc: the hook creates curiosity, the middle keeps changing expectations, and the ending earns a specific feeling appropriate to that story. Choose the intended feeling before blocking and assess the complete cut against it. Record the user's response and which beats produced it, so later builds reinforce effective choices rather than merely accumulating effects.

Use the 3D set as a place to travel through and act within. Compose near, middle, and far layers; let foreground objects, doorways, table edges, tools, and terrain disclose information as the camera changes position. Choose ground-level, low table, shoulder, profile, reverse, or overhead perspectives for a specific story purpose. Frame flat characters with appropriate front/profile/rear drawings and world-directed eyes, hands, feet, and accessories so their performance belongs to that space.

Sound design is part of the performance and perspective. Let proximity, direction, material, room/field ambience, and timing reinforce the current subject. Coordinate anticipation, contact, decision, and response with purposeful accents and music dynamics. A brief withdrawal can give the next action weight; atmosphere should connect transitions without becoming dead air. Preserve narration clarity and assess the combined moving picture and soundtrack, distinguishing level checks from listening.

Explore inventive but legible visual connections: following a prop into a reaction, revealing a person through foreground occlusion, matching a recurring object across time, or moving from a character's viewpoint into the mechanism. Choose the idea because it makes the story more compelling. Keep historical claims intact and preserve orientation; novelty is not a reason to introduce unsupported events or a confusing move.

Before calling the result polished, ask whether the shot makes the audience look at the intended subject, understand what changes, and feel why it matters. Judge that integrated experience against relevant reference footage, not a list of independently completed effects.

Clarity and continuity are necessary but insufficient. The diamond V2 review passed those checks while the user still found the result stiff and slow. Review dramatic momentum separately.

For each important beat, identify who wants what, the visible decision or test, and the response that changes the situation. Separate an action from its reaction so the latter has a cause. Avoid holding a completed pose through the rest of a sentence or returning to an object without changing its meaning. Preserve factual claims; do not invent dialogue, tests, or historical events to manufacture conflict.

Choose camera height, side, and path around the information being revealed. A move should uncover evidence, connect people, disclose a consequence, or give the viewer a useful point of view. Stage characters toward their world targets and use appropriate front, profile, or rear drawings; camera-facing cards alone do not create convincing interaction. Use foreground framing and occlusion to make travel through the world perceptible. Keep current symmetric-glide guidance, but distinguish anticipation, discovery travel, action holds, and decisive arrivals. Arbitrary tilt and constant movement are not substitutes for staging.

Do not default to whole bodies or complete groups at decisive moments. Briefly establish the relationship, then let a relevant face, hand, or prop dominate the frame. Make scrutiny, waiting, decision, relief, possession, and private reaction readable at the chosen scale. A close-up must earn its place through an action or changed intention; check prop alignment and caption clearance at that larger scale. For costly revisions, demonstrate this in a short moving excerpt before committing to another full render.

Use value, color, edge contrast, and selective depth of field to give one subject priority. Local bloom belongs to a motivated bright source or brief accent. Blanket glow, blur, saturation, and texture do not supply missing dramatic change. Coordinate camera, action, reaction, light, and sound around the same event.

The user's default color signature is a warm/golden opening that develops into cooler blue lighting as the reveal changes the viewer's understanding. Time the transition to that realization and its reaction. Preserve readable faces, hands, and evidence; use cooler shadow/background color and focused contrast rather than an indiscriminate blue wash. Coordinate the sound and music change with the same narrative turn. This is emotional lighting direction, not a requirement to invent elapsed time or nightfall.

Each shot brief must name that subject, the change the audience should notice, camera angle and movement, light/focus emphasis, the character's intention and response, and the sound cue. Treat these as one direction decision. Close framing alone does not satisfy the user's reference benchmark; judge the coordinated moving shot and its handoff.

The reviewer must name what each main move reveals and flag stretches where only framing changes. Also check whether the audience can identify a character's immediate intention and what changed after the action. A successful render or a comprehensible sequence is not an impact approval.
