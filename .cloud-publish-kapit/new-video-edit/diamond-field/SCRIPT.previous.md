# The planted diamond field

## Verified facts and sources

- In 1872, Philip Arnold and John Slack deceived investors with a planted gemstone field in Colorado. They bought uncut gems in London; mining engineer Henry Janin endorsed the site. Arnold collected $550,000 before expenses, and Slack received $50,000. Clarence King's team found gems only in disturbed ground, among other evidence of planting.
- Smithsonian, “The Great Diamond Hoax of 1872”: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-great-diamond-hoax-of-1872-2630188/
- Cross-check of the central planted-gemstone fraud: Colorado Geological Survey, Proceedings of the Fifteenth Forum on Geology of Industrial Minerals (1979): https://hermes.cde.state.co.us/islandora/object/co%3A27316/datastream/OBJ/download/Proceedings_of_the_Fifteenth_Forum_on_Geology_of_Industrial_Minerals___theme__industrial_minerals_in_Colorado_and_the_Rocky_Mountain_region__Golden__Colorado__June_13-15__1979.pdf

## Idea

Two con men fooled investors with real gemstones planted in a fake Colorado diamond field.

## Voiceover

Two men struck it rich by putting diamonds into the ground.

In 1872, they led investors to a remote spot in Colorado. Visitors picked up diamonds and rubies. A mining engineer approved it. The men walked away with hundreds of thousands of dollars.

But investigators noticed something odd: gems appeared only where someone had disturbed the ground.

The men had stocked the site with stones bought in London.

They weren’t mining diamonds. They were mining investors.
